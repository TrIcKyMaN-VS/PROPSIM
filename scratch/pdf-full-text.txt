How this system works
Most traders "journal" by screenshotting a win and moving on. That is a scrapbook, not a journal. This system is
built as three connected layers, and each layer feeds the next one. If you skip a layer, the one above it has
nothing to work with.
L A Y E R 	W H E N 	W H A T I T P R O D U C E S
Daily 	Before &
after every

Raw data — your plan, your trades, your mistakes and your emotional state,
recorded before memory rewrites them.

Monthly 	Last day of

you commit to next month.
The six ground rules
01 	Journal the same day, before you sleep. Memory decays within hours. The emotional truth — why you actually
clicked — decays even faster.
02 	Screenshot every trade. Mark your entry, your stop, your target and the level or footprint that justified it. No
screenshot, no entry in the journal.
03 	Write what you saw, not what you wish you saw. Nobody else has to read this. The moment you start writing
for an audience, the journal stops being useful.
04 	Separate process from outcome. A loss taken exactly by the model is a good trade. A win taken outside the
model is a bad trade that got paid. Grade both honestly.
05 	Never skip a red day. The days you least want to write about carry the most information. That is the entire
point of the red-day checklist.
06 	Reviews are not optional. The daily journal only collects evidence. The weekly and monthly reviews are where
that evidence becomes an edge.
How to use this workbook
Sections 1–3 explain each layer and what a strong answer looks like. At the end of each section you will find a
blank template you can copy into Notion, Obsidian, a spreadsheet or a paper notebook — whatever you will
actually open every day. The tool does not matter. The consistency does.

Part A — The pre-market plan
Written before the session opens. This is the document your post-session notes will grade you against, so it has
to be specific enough to be wrong.
1. Higher-timeframe context
Is price balanced or imbalanced? Where are we relative to yesterday's value area, the overnight range and the weekly open?
What did the last session leave unfinished?
2. Levels that matter today
List them — and next to each one, write why it matters. A level without a reason is decoration.
3. Scenarios, written as if / then
Two or three at most. "If price opens inside balance and rejects the high → I look for a short
on the retest, not the break." If you cannot write the scenario as a sentence, you do not have a plan — you have
a bias.
4. News & event risk
Time-stamped. Decide now whether you trade through it, before it, or not at all.
5. Risk parameters for the day
Risk per trade, maximum trades, maximum daily loss, and the point at which you close the platform. Numbers, not feelings.
6. State check — 1 to 10
Sleep, stress, and whether you are carrying anything from yesterday's session. Below a 5, size down or sit out. This single line
will explain more of your bad days than any chart ever will.
The test of a good plan: if you handed it to another trader in the mentorship, could they trade your session
without asking you a single question? If not, it is not finished.

Part B — Trade breakdown
One block per trade, every trade — winners, losers and scratches. Repeat this block as many times as you
traded.
T R A D E 0 1 	Setup name: ______________________ Result: _______ R
Insert the chart screenshot here — entry, stop, target and the level that justified them
Reasoning & logic
What was the setup, and what confirmed it? Context, level, order flow, effort vs result, and how it tied back to the pre-
market plan. Be specific enough that you could recognise this exact condition again six months from now.
Result vs expectation
What actually happened, and did it behave the way the model says it should? A trade can hit target and still behave wrong —
note that when it happens.
Mistakes & improvement
Entry timing, sizing, management, exit. Could this execution have been better, or was it clean? "No mistakes" is a valid
answer — but only if it is true.
Grade every trade on process, not P&L
A — By the model, well executed.
B — By the model, sloppy entry or exit.
C — Marginal setup, forced.
D — Outside the model entirely.
Track the grade next to the R. When your A-grades are profitable and your C and D grades bleed, you no longer need
convincing — you have proof.

Part C — The red-day checklist
Complete this only on days you close negative. Four questions, answered honestly, will tell you whether you
had a bad day or a bad process — and those two require completely different responses.
[ ] 1. Am I losing more today than I made on my last profitable day?
If yes, the problem is asymmetry, not accuracy. One red day should never erase several green ones. Check size and check
how long you stayed in the seat.
[ ] 2. Did I execute the setups following the model?
If yes — this was a cost of doing business, and you owe yourself nothing. If no, name the exact deviation. "I was impatient" is
not a deviation; "I entered on the break instead of waiting for the retest" is.
[ ] 3. Can I blame myself for risk management?
Did you oversize, add to a loser, move a stop, revenge trade, or take more trades than your plan allowed? This is the
question that separates a drawdown from a blow-up.
[ ] 4. Am I comfortable enough to go to sleep calm?
If not, write down exactly what is keeping you up. Nine times out of ten it is not the money — it is a rule you know you broke.
Name it here and it becomes next week's rule.
The verdict — write one line
Good process, bad outcome.
Model followed, risk respected, market simply did not
pay. No change required. Do not "fix" anything — that
is how a clean strategy gets destroyed.
Bad process.
One or more rules broken. This day goes straight into
the weekly review as raw material for a new rule.
Write the specific trigger, not the emotion.
Corrective action for tomorrow (one only): e.g. no trades in the first 10 minutes; maximum two setups; hard
stop after −2R.

Part D — Post-session notes
Written after the close, on every day — green, red or flat. This is where the day gets scored against the plan you
wrote before it started.
1. How accurate was my pre-market plan? ___ / 10
Did the market do what you expected? Which scenario played out, and which one did you not even consider? Being wrong
here is fine and useful. Being vague is not.
2. How much of my plan did I actually follow? ___ / 10
These two scores are the most important numbers in the whole journal. A high accuracy score with a low adherence score
means you can read the market and cannot follow yourself — that is an execution problem, and it is fixable in weeks. The
reverse is a strategy problem, and it takes longer.
3. Overall thought process after the session
Free writing. What the session felt like, where you hesitated, where you were sharp, what you noticed about the market that
is not in your model yet. Three to five honest sentences beat a page of tidy ones.
4. Add / remove / consider for tomorrow
One thing to add, one thing to remove, one thing to watch. Keep it to three — anything longer is a wish list, not an
adjustment.

From mistakes to rules
At the end of every week, reread all five daily journals in one sitting. You are not looking at P&L. You are looking
for repetition — the same mistake appearing on Tuesday and Thursday, or the same condition showing up
before your best entries.
01 	Read the week end to end. Highlight every repeated mistake and every repeated win condition. Once is noise.
Twice is a pattern. Three times is a rule waiting to be written.
02 	Turn each pattern into a rule. A rule must be written as a condition plus an action, and it must be specific
enough that someone else could apply it to your charts and get the same answer.
03 	Backtest the same week using those rules. Same days, same charts, replayed — but this time your new rules
are in force.
04 	Compare the backtested week against what you actually did. The gap between the two is the exact cost of
your undisciplined behaviour, in R, in writing.
05 	Keep, adjust or retire. A rule stays in "testing" until it has survived three separate weeks. Only then does it join
the model.
What a good rule looks like
Structure rule
When price is balanced, prefer entries on the breakout and retest — trade the transition
from balance into imbalance, not the middle of the range.
Order-flow rule
Before executing, when aggressive large trades show on the 1-minute, check effort vs
result across more than one 1-minute candle before accepting that one side is truly in
control.
Your rules will not look like these. These are examples from my own review, born out of my own mistakes.
Yours have to come from your daily journals — and they will not all be setup rules. A rule can be a time window
("no entries in the first 10 minutes of the session"), a risk protocol ("stop for the day after two consecutive
losses"), or a behavioural rule ("no trade without a written scenario"). If a mistake cost you money twice this
week, it has earned a rule.

Three questions to close the week
• Which rule would have saved the most R this week?

as skipped.

Winning trades
Once you have a full month of daily journals and four weekly reviews, sit down and audit the month in three
blocks: what won, what lost, and how you behaved. Start with the winners — not to celebrate them, but to find
out which part of your edge is actually repeatable.
Which setups made the most impact this month — and are they repeatable?
Rank your setups by net R contribution, not by hit rate. Then ask the harder question of each one: could I define its
conditions clearly enough to trade it again next month, or did it depend on a market state that has already passed?
Did I maximise my winning days over my losing days?
Compare total R made on green days against total R lost on red days. Your green days should be meaningfully larger. If they
are not, you are cutting winners early, holding losers long, or sizing up on exactly the wrong days.
What did my best trades have in common?
Time of session, market state, level type, order-flow confirmation, how long you waited before entering, your state score
that morning. Whatever repeats across your top five trades is the core of your edge — and it belongs in your rule book.
How did these wins affect my confidence and my process?
Did a good run make you sharper, or did it make you sloppy and oversized two days later? Winning streaks damage more
accounts than losing streaks do, because nobody inspects them.

Losing trades
Losses are only expensive if you fail to extract anything from them. This block decides whether this month's
losses were tuition or waste.
Were my losses within planned risk?
Go through every red trade and check the size against the plan. Did you oversize on any of them? Did you overtrade on
specific days? Mark the dates — overtrading almost always clusters around the same triggers.
Did I follow my rules on losing trades?
Split the losses into two piles: rule-following losses and rule-breaking losses. Add up the R in each. The first pile is the cost of
your edge. The second pile is the money you paid for nothing.
What patterns show up in my losing trades?
A particular time of the session? A particular day of the week? After a win, or after a loss? On days your state score was
below 5? After watching someone else's trade? Find the trigger, because the trigger is what you can control — the loss itself
is not.
Did I learn from my losses, or did I repeat the same mistakes?
Compare against last month's recap. If the same mistake appears in both, the problem is no longer knowledge — it is a
missing rule or a missing constraint. Build the constraint into your environment, not your willpower.

Overall — behaviour & progress
The last block is not about trades at all. It is about the trader. Answer these against last month, not against
some imaginary standard.
Did my discipline, consistency and mindset improve compared to last month?
Point to evidence, not feeling. Fewer rule breaks, fewer revenge trades, higher average adherence score, fewer days below a
5 on the state check.
Did I follow my rules more this month than last month?
Count the rule breaks in both months and compare directly. This is the single cleanest measure of progress in the entire
workbook, because it is immune to whether the market happened to suit you.
Was I consistent with journaling and weekly reviews — and did I act on them?
How many days did you journal out of the days you traded? How many weekly reviews did you complete? And did any rule
you wrote actually change your behaviour, or did it just sit in a document?
What one or two changes will I commit to next month?
Two maximum. Specific, measurable, and checkable in thirty days. "Be more patient" fails all three tests. "No entries before
9:45, no more than three trades per session" passes all three.

Compare this month against last month
Once you have two months of recaps written, put them side by side and compare your answers to the same
questions. This is where improvement stops being a feeling and becomes visible. Use an AI assistant for this step
— it will spot repetition across two documents faster than you will, and it has no reason to flatter you.
P R O M P T — C O P Y A N D P A S T E
You are my trading performance coach. Below are my monthly journal recaps for [MONTH A]
and [MONTH B], answering the same set of questions.
Compare them and tell me:
1. What measurably improved, with the numbers.
2. What stayed the same or got worse.
3. Which mistakes appear in both months — quote my own words back to me.
4. Which rules I said I would follow but the answers suggest I did not.
5. Whether my stated commitments from [MONTH A] actually showed up in [MONTH B].
6. The one or two highest-leverage changes for next month, ranked.
Be blunt and specific. Do not encourage me for the sake of it. If the evidence says I did
not improve, say so plainly.

What to do with the output
01 	Do not accept it at face value. The AI only sees what you wrote. If your journals are vague, the comparison will
be vague. Garbage in, encouragement out.
02 	Look for the repeated mistake first. Anything appearing in both months is your single highest-priority fix for
next month. Everything else waits.
03 	Convert the findings into rules, using the same condition → action format from the weekly review. A finding
that does not become a rule changes nothing.
04 	Save the comparison. Three of these back to back is the clearest record of development you will ever own as a
trader.

R E F E R E N C E
The rhythm & the numbers
Your journaling rhythm
W H E N 	T I M E N E E D E D 	W H A T Y O U D O
Before the open 	10–15 min 	Write the pre-market plan. Context, levels, scenarios, risk, state check.
After each trade 	2 min 	Screenshot and one line of reasoning while it is still fresh. Fill in the rest
after the close.
After the close 	15–20 min 	Trade breakdowns, red-day checklist if negative, post-session notes and
scores.
Weekend 	60–90 min 	Read the week, build rules, backtest the week with them, compare and
commit.
End of month 	90 min 	Full recap, month-over-month comparison, one or two commitments.
Metrics worth tracking
M E T R I C 	W H A T I T T E L L S Y O U
R multiple 	Result of a trade expressed in units of the risk you took. Reporting in R rather than
currency keeps the analysis honest across different account sizes.
Expectancy 	Average R per trade over a sample. The only number that answers "is this model actually
worth trading?"
Profit factor 	Gross R won divided by gross R lost. Below 1.0 you are paying the market for the
privilege.
Adherence score 	How much of your written plan you actually followed. Fully within your control — and it
usually leads the equity curve.
Discipline tax 	Total R lost specifically on rule-breaking trades. The fastest single number to improve.
Green vs red day ratio 	Average R on winning days against average R on losing days. Tells you whether your
asymmetry is right way round.
Thirty days from now, the value of this workbook will not be in any single entry. It will be in the pattern that
appears when you read a month of them in one sitting — the same mistake, the same hour, the same trigger,
in your own handwriting. That is the moment the journal starts paying you back.
